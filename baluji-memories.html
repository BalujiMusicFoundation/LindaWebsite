Baluji's Memories

Sitar virtuoso Baluji Shrivastav OBE, in conversation with Linda Shanson — small stories of a childhood in India, and the tastes that carry it still.

Every so often, Linda records Baluji talking through the memories that surface as they cook, eat, or simply sit and talk — small, unplanned conversations about the India he grew up in.

Recorded 28 July 2026.

A conversation

Childhood memories of food

Linda: What about these pakoras you used to eat as a child?

Baluji: Those pakoras used to be very small and round and very smooth, and I used to enjoy them so much. In Ajmer Blind School, we used to have the same kind of pakoras, and I used to look forward to Friday, when there was pakoras for refreshment after school.

So in this school I used to just imagine that I'm going to have nice pakoras. And when I came to Europe, I had these pakoras — big Punjabi-style pakoras. I hate them! So I don't know when I will get those pakoras again.

When my mother used to make pakoras, I used to eat so many of them, she used to say, “Leave something for kadhi.” She used to make this besan kadhi, and those pakoras should go in there.

Linda: Were they the size of a pea? Were they that small, or were they bigger?

Baluji: No — how can I say — size of a grape? No. Of a plum? No, that's too big.

Linda: Size of a cherry?

Baluji: Cherry, yeah, cherry — that's right. Size of a cherry, but round and flat.

Linda: Round and flat. And what did you have them with?

Baluji: Pakodi with potato, pakodi with spinach.

Linda: And what was in the pakodi — just besan?

Baluji: Yeah, just besan.

— Baluji Shrivastav OBE, recorded by Linda Shanson

A conversation

The shape and smell of India

Linda: Remembering the smell and the feeling of the village — which village is this?

Baluji: Khanpur.

Linda: Okay.

Baluji: And Usmanpur. There are two villages we used to transfer in between. So I do remember, kind of similar.

Each curve of the terrace and the smell of cow dung, because we didn't have the cement thing, that every day — or if not every day, then every alternate day — they have to put the cow dung everywhere, to smell good and to smell lovely. And the smell of the butter, ghee, coming out of the milk, was so strong, I could just feel like I'm there. Also, thatched.

And yeah, also the curves, all the holes on top of this — instead of shelves, we used to have these holes, made naturally by people themselves. We didn't have to hire any labourers for it, just had to do ourselves.

Also, in the houses where there was no thatch, we used to put wooden beams. And I could feel — I still feel, when I'm describing — I feel different smells of different places. So we used to travel from house to the fields, and we had different smells of different kinds of corns, chickpeas, or barley.

— Baluji Shrivastav OBE, recorded by Linda Shanson
